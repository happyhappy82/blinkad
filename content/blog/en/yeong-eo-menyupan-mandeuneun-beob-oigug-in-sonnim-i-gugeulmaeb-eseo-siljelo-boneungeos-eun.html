An English menu is often one of the first things a restaurant owner prepares for international customers. But having a translated menu inside does not mean a visitor can find the information needed to choose the restaurant.

Someone researching a visit may look at Maps, reviews, photographs, menu links, prices and opening hours first. Make the English menu available before the visit and keep it consistent with what the restaurant serves.

For restaurants, cafés, bars, bakeries and barbecue businesses, a useful menu explains more than the dish name. Ingredients, heat level, price, portions and order conditions can help a visitor assess whether the business suits their plans. Clear information does not guarantee that they will choose it.

How a customer might encounter the menu

The menu may first appear on a phone rather than on a restaurant table. One possible journey is:

  • Search for a nearby restaurant on Maps or a travel app.
  • Look through photographs of the business and food.
  • Read reviews, including ones in a language they understand.
  • Look for menu photographs or a menu link.
  • Assess prices and how straightforward ordering will be.
  • Visit and check the current in-store menu.

That is an illustrative sequence, not a universal customer path or a measured study. Make the useful information available on the profile and menu page as well as inside the restaurant.

Seven steps for preparing an English menu

1. Explain the food alongside the name

A customer familiar with Korean food may understand “kimchi jjigae.” A first-time visitor may need “kimchi stew with pork,” if that matches the recipe. Keep the recognizable name and add a description where useful:

  • Name alone: Doenjang jjigae.
  • With a description: soybean-paste stew with tofu and vegetables.
  • Name alone: Samgyeopsal.
  • With a description: grilled pork belly served with lettuce wraps.

Confirm the actual ingredients and accompaniments before using these examples. A description should not promise tofu, pork or lettuce wraps the restaurant does not serve.

2. State heat levels and relevant ingredients

A customer may need to know whether a dish contains an ingredient they avoid. Possible labels include:

  • Spicy / mildly spicy / not spicy.
  • Pork / beef / chicken / seafood; vegetarian only when verified.
  • Contains nuts / contains shellfish / contains dairy.

This is not a complete allergen list. Verify stocks, sauces, seasonings and preparation with the kitchen, including relevant cross-contact information. A vegetarian label or a photograph does not establish allergy suitability.

3. Highlight around five signature items

A “Recommended” section can give a first-time customer a manageable starting selection. Five is an editorial suggestion; the right number depends on the menu. Keep the full menu and ordering information available.

A barbecue business might highlight its signature cuts; a Korean restaurant might choose stews, bibimbap or bulgogi; a café might start with its own drinks and desserts. Use the actual offer and honest descriptions.

4. Make prices and ordering units clear

Explain per-person prices, minimum servings, separately charged rice and portion weights where they apply. For example:

  • Price per person.
  • Minimum order: two servings.
  • Rice not included.
  • 150 g per serving.

These are wording examples, not conditions every restaurant should adopt. A minimum of two servings is different from requiring two diners.

5. Match photographs to the current dishes

Customers may still see old menu photographs on Maps. Upload a current version and check that dish photographs use the right names. Remove outdated images you uploaded where appropriate; inaccurate customer uploads can be flagged, but you cannot assume every old photograph will disappear.

6. Update the profile's menu information

Google says eligible food and drink businesses can add menu items, descriptions and prices. The profile may also support menu photographs, PDFs and a menu link. Tools vary with the profile and provider. Allow time for updates to display and check the result customers can actually see.

7. Use dish names naturally in relevant review replies

If a review describes a particular dish, you can refer to it accurately in the reply. For example, “Thank you for trying our grilled pork belly and kimchi stew” fits a review that actually discusses those items. Do not repeat menu keywords in every reply or invent what the customer ordered.

Details to consider by business type

Barbecue restaurants

Explain the cut, cooking method, portion size, whether staff cook at the table and which accompaniments are included. Each detail should match the service.

Korean restaurants

Explain food types such as soup, stew and rice bowl, then describe how the meal is served. A category such as stew or rice bowl gives a first-time customer a useful starting point.

Cafés and bakeries

Describe signature items, decaffeinated choices, milk alternatives and takeaway where actually available. Verify ingredients instead of treating a milk substitution as a general allergy assurance.

Bars and izakaya

Explain the food and drinks, order requirements, any table charge and last-order times. Make additional costs clear before the customer orders.

Common mistakes

  • Publishing a machine translation without checking the language and facts.
  • Giving customers no clear starting selection.
  • Updating only the printed menu while leaving online photographs out of date.
  • Omitting prices, portions, heat levels or relevant ingredient information.
  • Preparing an English menu without making it available through the profile's menu link or photographs.

Frequently asked questions

Is a printed English menu enough?

A printed version helps inside the restaurant. For customers researching a visit, provide current online photographs, a menu link or profile menu information where available.

Does every item need an English translation?

A complete, accurate version is useful. You can begin with around five signature items that are easy to explain and show clearly, while considering the business's priorities, including item value. Make the full menu's prices and conditions available rather than leaving other orders unexplained.

Can we use machine translation?

Use it for a draft, then check the wording and the restaurant's actual names, ingredients, heat levels, allergens and portion units. Do not let a tool invent a recipe or dietary claim.

Do menu photographs on Maps help?

They can give customers useful information. Google provides menu-photo or PDF tools for eligible profiles. Keep your own uploads current and flag inaccurate customer-uploaded information where appropriate.
