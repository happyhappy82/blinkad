If you are commissioning a website for international customers, design mockups are only part of the decision. Ownership, ongoing maintenance and the way the site handles search will matter long after launch. These six checks can help you spot problems that could lead to an expensive rebuild later.

At BlinkAd, we work with businesses preparing to reach international customers. Website contracts come up often in those conversations.

Some owners have told us about difficult experiences with providers, especially when they wanted to make changes or move their site. Those accounts are a reason to ask clear questions before signing.

You may be considering a multilingual website or a landing page as part of your own international marketing.

Before choosing a provider, work through these six points and get the answers in writing.

1. Who controls the domain and hosting accounts?

Start here. If the provider registers the domain in its own name and controls the account, changing providers may put your continued use of the address at risk. Ask who the registrant and account holder will be, who has access, and how control can be transferred. Put the agreed arrangement in the contract. “We manage everything for you” does not answer who controls the accounts.

2. Are the maintenance terms specific?

A low build price can become less attractive if every small change attracts an unexpected charge. Ask for three things: the recurring maintenance fee, the changes included in that fee, and the rate or pricing method for work outside that scope. If the quotation omits them, request a written breakdown.

3. Will you see the mobile design before approval?

Consider a visitor checking your business on a phone while travelling. A desktop mockup does not show whether that experience works. Ask for a mobile preview rather than relying on the word “responsive.” Can the visitor open the menu, call the business and reach the booking option comfortably? Check the actual target-language screens as well.

4. Can the provider specify the search and AI-search work?

“SEO included” is too vague to compare. Ask what the provider will deliver for conventional search and, where relevant, answer engine optimization (AEO) or generative engine optimization (GEO). Our starting checklist includes:

  • A way to manage each page's title and description metadata.
  • Main content published as readable page text, rather than only embedded in images.
  • The ability to publish useful answers to customers' actual questions, including an FAQ section where appropriate.
  • Google Search Console and analytics setup, with account access and measurement scope agreed.

Clear text gives readers and search systems accessible information about the business. Images can support it; they should not carry all the essential information on their own. These items support a usable, discoverable site, but they do not guarantee rankings, AI citations or inquiries. Google does not require a special AI schema or a fixed FAQ format for its AI search features.

5. Is there a relevant, working example in the portfolio?

Look beyond attractive screenshots. Has the provider built sites for a similar type of business? Are those sites still operating? A site that has been maintained for a year gives you a useful view of the provider's ongoing work. Where possible, try its mobile navigation, language options and contact paths yourself.

6. What will be handed over when the contract ends?

Agree on the exit process before you need it. Our minimum discussion list is control of the domain, hosting access, the site's files or a usable backup, and administrator accounts. The deliverable depends on the platform: a hosted website service may provide an export rather than its underlying software, and third-party licenses may have transfer limits. Specify what is included, what can actually be moved, any charges, and the handover timing in the contract.

Five questions to send when comparing quotations

Send the same questions to two or three providers so you can compare their answers:

1. Who will be the registrant or account holder for the domain and hosting, and what access will we have?

2. What is the recurring maintenance fee, and which changes does it cover?

3. Can we manage titles and descriptions for individual pages?

4. What can we change ourselves through the administration screen?

5. What will we receive, and how will access be transferred, when the contract ends?

If a provider cannot give clear answers, resolve those gaps before signing, however appealing the price may be.

Frequently asked questions

Why do quotations vary so much?

Differences can reflect the amount of custom design, content work, search setup and ongoing support included. Compare scope rather than assuming that a higher or lower price tells you the quality. If a quote is inexpensive, ask which work and ongoing costs it leaves out.

The provider was recommended by a friend. Do we still need a written quotation?

Yes. Document these six points even if the introduction came through someone you trust. Written terms remain useful when staff, responsibilities or the relationship change later.

Do we really need to consider AI search now?

Customers can encounter business information through services such as ChatGPT and Google's AI answers. A website may stay in use for years, so it is sensible to ask whether its content can be accessed, understood and updated as search changes. That is a planning consideration, rather than a promise of immediate AI referrals.

These are the points we encourage owners to check when planning a website project.
