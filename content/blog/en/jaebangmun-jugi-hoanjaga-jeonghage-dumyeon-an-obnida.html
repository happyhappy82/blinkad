When patients do not return, the first explanations are often price or satisfaction. Another possibility deserves attention: they may be putting off a visit because they do not know when to come back. Treatment effects and suitable intervals vary, and a patient may find the timing difficult to judge. A useful reminder starts by making the clinic's recommendation clear.

Uncertainty makes it easier to put things off

Different providers may recommend different intervals for what appears to be the same treatment. Patients may not have enough information to understand which advice applies to them.

When the timing is unclear, the decision can slip down the list. Other commitments intervene, and someone who eventually wants an appointment may choose whichever clinic has space that day.

Giving a clear recommendation in advance makes it easier to plan a return visit. Without that guidance, postponement may reflect uncertainty rather than dissatisfaction.

Take responsibility for explaining the timing

We suggest thinking of a return reminder as the clinic or business taking responsibility for recommending when to come back. The patient or customer still decides whether to book.

  • No clear recommendation: uncertainty can lead to postponement and a lost return visit.
  • A clear recommendation: the customer has a reference point for planning the next visit.

Before polishing the wording, agree on the appropriate timing. Otherwise, staff will not know whom to contact or when.

How to plan the reminders

1. Write down the recommended interval for each service

Begin with the three services you provide most often. Trying to document everything at once can delay getting started.

Use the interval the clinician or responsible practitioner actually recommends. The task is to record existing advice, rather than have a marketing tool set a new treatment schedule.

2. Begin with services that have shorter return intervals

For this workflow, prioritize the frequency of appropriate return visits rather than revenue alone.

With shorter intervals, customers face that decision more often, giving the business more opportunities to provide timely guidance. With a service repeated only every few years, any effect of the reminder process will take longer to observe.

Starting priorityType of service
FirstServices or maintenance appropriately repeated at intervals of several weeks
SecondServices appropriately repeated at intervals of several months
ThirdServices with intervals of a year or longer

3. Discuss the next visit before the current one ends

Explain the recommended timing while the patient or customer is still with you, and record it. Ending with “We'll be in touch” simply leaves another task for someone to remember later.

4. Arrange for reminders to be sent at the recorded time

A process that depends on someone preparing a list and sending every message manually can stop during a busy week. If no one catches up on the missed reminders, the people due that week may be left out entirely.

If someone does not return, change the conversation

Sending the same reminder repeatedly can lead someone to block the messages.

Instead, make the next conversation about understanding why they have not returned. Price, disappointment with the result, inconvenient timing and simply forgetting call for different responses.

What we observed in our own work

Review activity is one of the things we monitor each month. In the original August 2026 article, we reported more than 2,000 accumulated reviews across the profiles we managed.

We repeatedly noticed a connection between post-visit contact, return visits and later reviews. Customers who heard from the business sometimes returned and then left a review. These observations do not establish cause and effect; visitor numbers and other factors can also affect review activity.

Another recurring problem was incomplete coverage. Inquiries arrived through three or four channels, but return reminders went out through only one. People who first contacted the business elsewhere were absent from the sending list. Even well-chosen timing cannot help someone who never receives the message: reaching only half of an eligible list leaves the other half out.

How this applies to different businesses

Dermatology and regular skincare services: Where a clinician or practitioner recommends a shorter return interval, these can be a practical place to begin.

Hair salons, nail salons and waxing businesses: Services with an established maintenance cycle can suit reminders that help customers plan ahead.

Semi-permanent makeup and skincare salons: Touch-ups and a planned series of sessions provide clear occasions to discuss the next appointment.

Plastic surgery: Repeat elective procedures may be far apart, so this particular repeat-service reminder approach can have limited value. Handling new inquiries may deserve more attention. Necessary postoperative checks and aftercare follow the clinician's plan and are a separate matter.

Frequently asked questions

Will customers feel pressured if we contact them?

A pushy message can have that effect. A useful reminder explains the recommended timing without pressing someone to book. Respect the person's contact preferences.

What if the interval varies from person to person?

Record the practitioner's usual recommendation, then mark individual exceptions separately. The system should reflect those decisions rather than replace them.

Which channel should we use?

Begin with the channel the person already used to speak with you. A message from an unfamiliar number may be easier to overlook.

What if there is no response?

Avoid repeating the same message. If further contact is appropriate, ask whether there is a reason they have not returned.

We do not know how to choose the timing.

Start by writing down the recommendation the clinician or practitioner already gives at the end of the visit. You do not need to invent a new interval for the software.

Make the next step clear

Useful return-visit management means helping someone understand when to come back, rather than trying to persuade them repeatedly.

  • Record the recommended interval for each service, beginning with the three most common.
  • Start with services that have shorter appropriate intervals.
  • Discuss and record the next visit during the current appointment.
  • Arrange for the reminder to go out at the agreed time.
  • If someone has not returned, understand the reason.
  • Use the channel they already used to contact you.

The final point is easy to miss. If the sending list contains only one channel's customers, people who contacted you elsewhere can still be overlooked.
