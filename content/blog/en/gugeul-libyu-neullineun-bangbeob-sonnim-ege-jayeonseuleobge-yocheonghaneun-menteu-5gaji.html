Asking for a review can feel awkward, especially when you are busy serving customers. A clear, optional invitation is often easier than trying to persuade someone on the spot. Here are five ways to invite an honest Google review, followed by simple link, message and QR-code ideas.

Why Do Google Reviews Matter?

When someone looks for a nearby restaurant or salon, reviews can help them understand the experience others have had. A high review count is not the only reason a business appears in the results.

Google describes three main local-ranking considerations:

ConsiderationWhat it means
RelevanceHow well the business matches what someone is looking for.
DistanceHow far the business is from the searcher’s known or estimated location.
ProminenceHow well known the business is, including information such as relevant links, reviews and ratings.

You can improve accurate information and customer service, but you do not directly control customers’ reviews or Google’s ranking. The original article quoted 87% for review reading and suggested that fewer than ten reviews sharply reduces trust, without identifying the matching survey question and year. Those are not established current percentages or universal review-count thresholds. Nor does more visibility automatically mean a completed visit or sale.

A genuine review can help another customer understand the business long after it was written. Treat it as the customer’s account, rather than advertising copy you can commission or control.

Make It an Invitation, Without the Pressure

You are offering a customer a way to share their experience, not asking them to endorse a predetermined message.

  • Offer the opportunity neutrally, rather than choosing only people who say they are satisfied.
  • Make the link easy to find. Someone who wants to write a review can do so later without searching for the right business.
  • Make the choice comfortable. Do not pressure a person to write while on the premises, ask for a particular rating or tell them which words to include.

Five Natural Invitations for Different Businesses

Adapt these to the situation and your normal voice. Each should remain optional and unconnected to any gift, discount, extra service or treatment decision.

1. A Restaurant: After the Meal

“Thank you for visiting. If you’d like to share your experience on Google, our review link is here. You’re welcome to use it whenever it suits you.”

Do not ask the customer to name a particular dish or select a rating. A QR code can make the correct destination clear without holding up payment.

2. A Café: A Simple Invitation

“Thanks for stopping by. If you’d like to leave an honest review of your visit, you can find our Google link here.”

If you are running a tasting or promotion, keep it independent of any review. A free cookie must not become a reward or an implied exchange for posting feedback.

3. A Hair Salon: At the End of the Visit

“Thank you for coming in today. If you’d like to share how the visit went, our Google review link is here. It’s entirely up to you.”

Do not connect an extra scalp-care service or future benefit to the review. Let the customer choose the content rather than asking them to include the service name.

4. A Clinic: Keep Feedback Separate from Care

“Thank you for visiting. If you choose to share feedback about your experience, the public review link is here. You do not need to include any private health information.”

A public review invitation should never affect access to care or depend on a positive satisfaction answer. Do not request a procedure name, diagnosis or treatment outcome. A clinic must also consider the privacy and medical-advertising requirements that apply to its own use of reviews; this example is not a determination of compliance.

5. A Gym or Academy: After Someone Has Had Time to Use the Service

“You’ve been with us for a little while now. If you’d like to share your experience, you can use our Google review link. Please write whatever you feel is useful.”

The original example used one month; choose a time that makes sense for the actual service. Do not make the invitation conditional on satisfaction or ask for the name of a particular class or machine.

The useful pattern is a thank-you, an optional invitation and an easy route. Leave the rating, wording and timing to the customer. No reward or screening question is needed.

Inviting Reviews by Message

If messaging is an appropriate, permitted contact route for that customer, a short invitation can let them choose a convenient time. Prepare the correct direct link first; do not send repeated reminders to someone who has declined.

Find the Review Link

1. Sign into the account with access to the correct business and find its profile.

2. Open the available option to request or get more reviews. Labels can vary between Search, Maps and devices.

3. Copy the business’s direct review link, or use the QR option where offered.

4. Check that the destination identifies the right business. A reviewer needs a Google Account; opening the link does not submit a review.

SMS and KakaoTalk Starting Points

Replace the brackets with real business details, and use the message only where it is appropriate to contact the customer.

SMS example

[Business name] Thank you for visiting today. If you’d like to share an honest review of your experience, you can use this Google link: [review link]. It’s optional, and we appreciate your feedback.

KakaoTalk example

Thank you for visiting [business]. If you choose to share your experience on Google, our review link is here: [review link]. Please use it whenever convenient and write in your own words. Thank you.

Choose a reasonable, non-intrusive time rather than targeting only customers at their “most satisfied” moment. Avoid making the request feel like a condition of payment or service. The original timing video (Korean) provides further context, subject to current policy.

Make the Review Link Easy to Find with a QR Code

A QR code saves someone from searching for the business themselves. It does not guarantee they will write a review, and it should not be used to watch or pressure them while they do.

Prepare and Check the Code

1. Get the correct direct review link from the business profile.

2. Use Google’s available QR option or a suitable generator, such as the Naver QR service referenced in the original article.

3. If using a generator, enter the correct review link and create the code.

4. Test the destination and readability before printing. Check it again if the business listing or destination changes.

Places Where a Code Can Be Convenient

PlacementPractical use
CounterA small, clearly labeled stand customers can choose to notice without being stopped during payment.
TableA card saying “Share your experience if you’d like,” without limiting the request to people who enjoyed the meal.
Entrance or waiting areaA discreet sign identifying the business’s Google review link.
ReceiptA code printed where the POS setup supports it, with no reward attached.

Requests That Cross the Policy Line

Google permits neutral invitations for genuine experiences. The way you ask still matters: “honest feedback” does not make an incentive or a selected-positive-review process acceptable.

Avoid these practices: keep the review independent and voluntary.
No incentives: do not offer money, discounts, gifts or free services for posting, changing or removing a review.
No fabricated or conflicted reviews: do not recruit yourself, staff, family or others with a conflict of interest to improve the rating.
No attacks on competitors: do not place false or conflicted negative reviews on another business.
No positive-only screening or dictated content: do not steer unhappy customers away from public reviews or require a star rating, keyword, staff name or service name.
No bought review output: a paid service must not supply purchased or fabricated customer reviews. Administrative help with a genuine process is different from buying the reviews themselves.

A fair request gives a genuine customer room to describe the experience in their own words, including criticism. The customer can also choose not to respond.
