A visitor can feel uncertain when staff struggle to explain an order, even if the restaurant has an English menu. Waiting, payment and takeaway instructions are part of the experience too.

Staff do not need fluent English for every routine exchange. Start with the situations that come up often and agree on short, accurate phrases the team can use.

In our work reviewing business profiles and customer feedback, we recommend keeping menu descriptions, waiting instructions, payment guidance and review replies consistent. The online information and the explanation at the counter should agree.

Why service can become part of a review

A customer's review may cover more than the food: whether ordering was straightforward, prices were clear, staff explained the options and checkout worked.

For someone who does not speak Korean, a short explanation can make the next step easier to understand. Staff can communicate helpfully without delivering a polished speech.

Unclear gestures, prices or menu explanations can also leave a poor impression. Preparing the information helps the team respond to those situations; it does not guarantee a particular rating.

Keep the manual short enough to use

A long manual is difficult to consult during service. These seven situations are a practical starting point:

  • Welcoming customers and seating them.
  • Explaining a wait.
  • Recommending a signature dish.
  • Explaining heat level and ingredients.
  • Explaining additional orders and minimum-order rules.
  • Payment and takeaway.
  • Thanking customers and inviting honest reviews.

Adapt the list to the questions your own customers ask. Seven is a starting framework, not a measured claim that it covers every service need.

1. Welcoming and seating customers

A friendly greeting, a party size and a clear direction are often enough to begin:

  • Hello, welcome! How many people are in your party?
  • A table for two?
  • Please wait a moment.
  • This way, please.
  • Please take a seat here.

Choose phrases staff are comfortable saying. Practice a short phrase until staff can use it comfortably during service.

2. Explaining the wait

If there is a wait, give the best estimate you can and update it when circumstances change. The figures below are examples to adapt, not a fixed promise:

  • The estimated wait is about 10 minutes.
  • We expect your table to be ready soon.
  • Please wait outside for a moment.
  • We'll let you know when your table is ready.
  • Would you like to wait?

Use the instructions that match your waiting area and notification process. “Sorry for the wait” is enough when a brief apology is appropriate.

3. Recommending a signature dish

A customer unfamiliar with the menu may appreciate a short description or recommendation. Use only statements that fit the actual dish:

  • This is our most popular dish.
  • This is a good choice for a first visit.
  • This dish isn't spicy.
  • This dish is spicy.
  • This dish is good for sharing.

For a barbecue restaurant, café, Korean restaurant or bar, preparing descriptions of around three signature items is a useful starting exercise. Do not label an item “most popular” without a basis for that statement.

4. Explaining ingredients and heat level

Customers may need to know about pork, seafood, nuts, dairy or other ingredients. These phrases are examples, not a complete allergen list or a recipe description:

  • It's spicy.
  • It's mildly spicy.
  • It isn't spicy.
  • It contains pork.
  • It contains seafood.
  • It contains nuts.
  • We can make it less spicy.

Confirm the recipe, stock, sauces and preparation with the kitchen. Promise a change only if the kitchen can make it. When a customer raises an allergy, check accurate ingredient and cross-contact information rather than guessing from the visible ingredients or using “less spicy” as a safety assurance.

5. Explaining order conditions

Minimum portions, one-item-per-person rules, last orders and separately charged rice should be clear before the order is placed. Adapt these examples to the restaurant:

  • The minimum order is two servings.
  • Please order one dish per person.
  • Rice is not included.
  • Last orders are at 9 p.m.
  • This dish serves two people.

The menu and staff should give the same portion, price and ordering information. The example time and rules above are not recommendations to introduce those conditions.

6. Payment and takeaway

Explain available payment methods and takeaway options simply. Use a phrase only when it accurately describes the service and complies with the business's obligations:

  • You can pay by card.
  • Cash only, please.
  • Takeaway is available.
  • Takeaway isn't available for this item.
  • Please pay at the counter.
  • We can split the payment.

A phrase list does not justify refusing an accepted card or promising a payment option the shop has not enabled. If the business accepts WOWPASS, Alipay or WeChat Pay, staff should know the actual method and how to explain an available alternative.

7. Thanking customers and inviting reviews

Keep the invitation voluntary and neutral. Ask about a genuine experience without selecting only customers who seem pleased:

  • Thank you for visiting us.
  • We hope you enjoyed your meal.
  • You're welcome to share your honest experience in a Google review.
  • Thank you. Have a great day!

Do not offer a discount or gift in exchange for a review, pressure customers to post, or ask for a particular rating or wording. The invitation should welcome honest feedback.

Review replies are part of customer service

When a review raises a useful point, respond in the customer's language where you can do so accurately. Adapt the response to what they actually wrote:

  • Thank you for visiting. We're glad you enjoyed the food.
  • Thank you for your review. We hope to welcome you again.
  • We're sorry about the difficulty. We'll review what happened with our team.
  • Thank you for trying our signature dish.

A short, relevant reply is enough. Do not repeat an unrelated template or promise follow-up that the team will not carry out.

A practical approach to staff training

Start with a phrase card staff can consult during service:

  • Choose around 20 frequently needed phrases.
  • Place them at the counter, near the kitchen or in a staff area.
  • Organize them by situation rather than relying entirely on memorization.
  • Use actual customer questions or review feedback to improve the wording.
  • Agree on consistent descriptions of the signature dishes.

Short practice sessions with real ordering situations can help staff use the phrases with more confidence. The kitchen should remain the source for recipe and preparation information.

Details owners sometimes miss

  • Staff give different descriptions of the same dish.
  • Heat levels and ingredients are guessed.
  • The wait is unclear or an outdated estimate is left uncorrected.
  • Payment options are not explained before they are needed.
  • A relevant question in a foreign-language review goes unanswered.
  • The profile's menu information differs from what staff say.

Frequently asked questions

Can staff help international customers without fluent English?

They can prepare for many routine situations with short phrases for seating, ordering, checkout and thanks. More complex questions need reliable language support and the right staff member, particularly for ingredients and allergies.

Can we ask customers for a Google review?

You can invite a voluntary review of a genuine experience. Do not offer incentives, pressure customers, request a rating or selectively ask only for positive feedback.

Must we reply in English?

A short, accurate reply in the reviewer's language can be helpful. If you cannot reliably reply in Japanese or Chinese, a polite, understandable English response may be an option. Address the actual point raised.

Where should staff keep the phrases?

Near the counter, POS or staff work area, where the person helping a customer can consult them quickly.
