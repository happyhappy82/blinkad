Someone may walk past your shop every day without knowing what you offer. For a café, salon or neighborhood service, the people living and working nearby are worth understanding before you spend money reaching a much wider audience. Hyperlocal marketing starts with those customers and the information that would help them visit. A one-kilometer circle is a useful place to begin thinking, though your actual catchment depends on the business and the streets around it.

What Is Hyperlocal Marketing?

Hyperlocal marketing focuses on a small geographic area around a business. That might mean a few hundred meters, a neighborhood or roughly one to two kilometers. Rather than targeting all of Gangnam, for example, you might plan around people near Exit 3 of Yeoksam Station.

Think of an office worker looking for lunch nearby, a resident searching for Pilates in Yeoksam-dong, or a family choosing a café for the weekend. Each has a different reason to visit, but all need useful local information. A small business can respond to that need without trying to advertise across an entire city.

Why Start with a One-Kilometer Area?

One kilometer may be about a 12-to-15-minute walk for some adults on an easy route. Hills, crossings, mobility needs and the actual path can change that considerably. Use it as an initial planning distance, rather than a universal limit on how far people will walk.

The Korean article described a Mapo bakery spending roughly KRW 800,000 a month on Seoul-wide Instagram advertising. It reported that over 90% of customers lived or worked within 800 meters, and that narrowing the target area to one kilometer halved spending while visits rose 20%. The underlying customer and campaign records were not supplied. These are figures reported in the original account, rather than a validated experiment or an expected return.

Nearby customers may have more opportunities to return. Whether they do depends on the product, service, convenience and experience. The practical question is where your regular customers come from and what would make a visit easier.

Five Ways to Reach Customers Nearby

1. Make Your Google Business Profile Useful

A Google Business Profile is one place customers can find the details they need when searching for a nearby business. Start with a clear picture of the business as it operates today.

  • Check the address, phone number and current opening hours.
  • Show the entrance, interior and representative products or menu items. Ten useful photos is a suggested starting collection, not a Google ranking requirement.
  • Select the most accurate category for the business.
  • Consider a weekly update when there is useful news to share; frequency alone does not guarantee better ranking.
  • Respond promptly to genuine questions through the features available to your profile and region.

2. Explain What You Offer in the Neighborhood

A location and a service together often describe what a customer needs more clearly than either one alone. Examples include “nail salon in Hapjeong,” “brunch café in Yeonnam-dong” and “personal training in Sinsa-dong.” These are translated examples, not newly measured search-volume findings.

  • Write website or blog content that explains the actual service and neighborhood.
  • Mention your location naturally in the profile description, without inserting unrelated search phrases.
  • Include useful directions from nearby stations, schools, parks or other landmarks.
The original article described a Seongsu flower shop moving from broad “Seoul florist” content to neighborhood topics such as flower delivery near Ttukseom Station and Seongsu flower workshops. It reported more than three times as many Google Maps search impressions after three months, alongside more walk-ins. No underlying report was provided to separate the content work from other changes.

3. Take Part in Local Communities

People often learn about nearby businesses through neighbors. Share something useful before asking neighbors to buy from you.

  • Online: consider relevant Naver cafés, Karrot neighborhood communities or local Band groups, following each community’s business-posting rules.
  • Offline: take part in local events, collaborate with nearby businesses, or support a suitable neighborhood gathering.
  • Be clear about your connection to the business. Share helpful information without posing as an unaffiliated resident or customer.

4. Consider Radius Targeting in Google Ads

Paid location targeting can help concentrate a campaign near a business. It is not a precise fence that guarantees every person seeing the ad is inside your circle.

  • For eligible campaign types, select an available area or radius around a location. Google’s current minimum radius is one kilometer; availability and privacy thresholds apply, and Hotel Ads do not support radius targeting.
  • A one-kilometer radius can be a starting test where suitable. Review the campaign’s presence versus presence-or-interest setting and actual geographic performance before adjusting the area.
  • Consider lunch or after-work demand when setting schedules. Bid adjustments depend on campaign and bidding strategy; they are not universally available or necessary.

5. Invite Genuine Customer Reviews

Reviews can help another local customer understand what a visit is like. Make it easy to leave an honest account without choosing the rating or wording.

  • Offer genuine customers an optional Google review link or QR code.
  • Read the feedback and reply when you can answer a question, acknowledge a concern or provide a helpful update.
  • Let customers describe their own experience. Do not ask them to include phrases such as “local regular” or “close to home” to influence the review.
The Korean article described a one-person Gangnam salon offering a free treatment on a future visit in return for a review, then reporting over 20 additional reviews in two months and improved map visibility. That incentive should not be copied: Google prohibits free services, discounts or other rewards for reviews, even when an honest review is requested. The reported results also lack supporting records. Use an optional, unrewarded invitation instead.

What Should You Measure?

Choose a few measures that let you see how customers move from finding the business to contacting or visiting it. Keep the meaning of each number clear.

Measures worth checking: use a consistent reporting period and note any changes in ads, hours or services.
Business Profile views and search terms: discovery measures, not counts of people who entered the shop.
Direction requests: a sign of possible interest in visiting, rather than confirmed arrivals.
Call-button clicks: Google counts clicks on the button, which do not establish answered calls or bookings.
Review volume and ratings: useful feedback to read alongside the actual service experience.
Advertising click-through and conversion rates: interpret them using the campaign’s area settings and the specific conversion action being counted.

Compare the same measures each month and look into any meaningful changes. To assess sales, add booking, visit or transaction records you are entitled to use. Profile data may include both organic and advertising activity, so it does not always isolate the effect of unpaid work.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which businesses might benefit?

Restaurants, cafés, salons, clinics, academies, laundries, gyms and florists can all have local demand. Services used regularly may suit a neighborhood focus. A wedding venue, funeral venue or other specialist business may draw customers from much farther away, so check the actual catchment before narrowing your marketing.

Can I begin with almost no advertising budget?

Yes. An eligible business can manage a Google Business Profile without paying Google a profile fee. You can also prepare useful local content and take part in communities that allow business participation. Those activities still take time. If you later try paid ads, use a budget and area appropriate to the business, and check the results before expanding.

How long should I allow?

The original article suggested looking for early changes in two to four weeks, then assessing a broader picture after one to three months. Treat these as observation windows, not guaranteed growth dates. A three-month trial may give you more context than a few days, but changes in demand, verification and competition can affect what you see.

You do not need a complex plan to begin. Learn where your customers come from, make the business easy to understand when they search, and keep the information accurate.
