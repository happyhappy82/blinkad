“Do I need a website before I can start marketing the shop?”

You can begin marketing an eligible local business without building a website first. A useful Google Business Profile can answer many of the questions a customer has before visiting. Whether it is enough depends on what you sell, how people contact you and how much detail they need.

Why You Can Begin Without a Website

Think about someone looking for lunch near Gangnam Station or a salon in their neighborhood. They may begin with a map listing, check the hours, look at photos and decide whether to call. A website can support that journey, but it is not the only place they find the business.

Google Business Profile lets eligible businesses manage information on Search and Maps without a Google profile fee. It can show the name, location or service area, hours, photos and reviews. It offers a useful public introduction, while a website gives you more control over detailed information and the customer journey.

The original article cited Google’s mobile-search research. Its 76% finding concerned people searching on smartphones for something nearby who visited a business within a day. The underlying study was conducted in May 2016 with US smartphone users, including 634 local searchers from a 1,000-user sample. It does not mean 76% of people searching today will visit your particular shop, or establish that profile work is always more efficient than a website.

Five Things a Business Profile Can Help You Do

Registration is the first step. The useful work is keeping the information and available contact options clear.

1. Show Essential Business Information

Customers can find your actual business name, location, phone number where provided, opening hours and category. Display depends on the query, profile status and local results; adding a listing does not guarantee a pin or top position for every service search.

2. Show the Place and What You Offer

Clear photos of the entrance, interior and representative products help customers understand a visit. The original article called the photo-related benefit a 35% higher click-through rate. Google’s July 2016 article actually reported 35% more clicks for listings with photos, citing December 2014 Google/Oxera research. That historical comparison is not a current CTR estimate or proof of a causal sales increase.

The Korean article described a neighborhood snack shop posting photos of its tteokbokki and receiving more comments from customers who had seen them after a month. No supporting visit records were supplied. The example illustrates a useful photo subject, rather than a verified growth result.

3. Share Useful Updates

Where available, posts can explain a new menu item, event or temporary change. They are reviewed and their public visibility depends on status and Google’s display. You can begin sharing relevant news without maintaining a separate blog.

4. Read and Respond to Reviews

A verified business can reply to reviews where supported. A helpful response acknowledges the actual feedback, gives useful context and protects private information. It is a customer-service habit, without a guaranteed increase in trust or ranking from every reply.

5. Connect Customers with the Next Step

A phone number can enable a call button, and supported booking features can link to an appropriate destination. Availability varies. A click is not a completed call or confirmed reservation; make sure the route customers reach is working and suitable for the service.

A profile can cover discovery, basic information and some contact actions. Check the features available to your business, and add a website when customers need information or functions the profile does not provide.

What Do Your Customers Need Before They Visit?

Use the examples below to identify the information customers need. No industry label alone proves that a website is unnecessary.

Businesses with Relatively Simple Local Questions

  • Restaurants and cafés: accurate location, hours, current menu information and photos can answer many first questions.
  • Hair and nail salons: service information, representative work and a working inquiry or booking route may provide a starting point.
  • Laundries and repair shops: opening hours, location and contact details can help a customer take the next step.
  • Neighborhood pharmacies: accurate opening hours and location can be especially useful. Keep regulated service and product information within applicable rules.
  • Local academies and tutoring services: an eligible in-person business may begin with a profile, adding detailed course information where prospective students need it.

Businesses That Often Need More Detail or Functionality

  • Online shops: customers need an appropriate shopping and payment route. An online-only business is not eligible for a Business Profile simply because it sells products.
  • Professional services: legal, accounting or consulting customers may need detailed service information and relevant credentials or work examples.
  • Franchises and multiple locations: a website can explain the brand and help people choose the correct branch.
  • B2B companies: buyers may need company information, specifications or documents that a short profile cannot explain well.
Where the business is eligible and serves local customers, a profile can complement a website. It is not compulsory for every business model, and lacking a profile does not mean a website is categorically excluded from local search.

Three Steps to Begin

Set aside time to prepare the basics. Thirty minutes may be a useful initial allowance, with verification, content review and further setup taking longer.

Step 1: Add or Claim the Correct Profile

  • Sign into the Google Account that should retain appropriate business access.
  • Look for an existing listing before adding a new one, and use the actual public business name.
  • Choose the most accurate available category.
  • Check the real address and map pin, or the appropriate service-area setup.
  • Follow the verification methods Google offers. They may include recorded or live video as well as phone, email or mail; you cannot assume any particular option is available.

Step 2: Prepare Useful, Accurate Details

  • Check regular and special opening hours.
  • Provide a suitable business phone number where appropriate.
  • Explain the business clearly within the 750-character description limit, without unrelated keyword lists.
  • Prepare a small photo collection, such as one exterior, two interior and two product images. Five is a planning suggestion rather than a Google ranking threshold.
  • Add services or menu information through the features available to the business.
Which photos should I begin with?

Show the entrance with the sign visible, the actual interior and representative products or menu items. Use clear, well-lit photos that reflect the real place. A smartphone can be sufficient; the customer needs to understand what they will find.

Step 3: Share One Useful Update

  • Open the post options available to the profile and choose a suitable Update, Event or Offer.
  • Use an appropriate photo and a short message with actual information a customer needs.
  • Submit the post and check its status before assuming it is visible to customers.
One or two posts a week may be a manageable schedule when there is worthwhile news. A menu change or temporary closure can be useful; posting frequency alone does not guarantee more exposure.
Invite genuine customers to share honest feedback if they choose. Do not recruit family or others with a conflict of interest, offer incentives or select only people likely to be positive. The original three-to-five-review suggestion is not a trust or ranking threshold.

Start with the Information Customers Need

A missing website does not mean you have to postpone every online marketing task. For an eligible local business, an accurate Business Profile can be a practical place to begin while you assess what else customers need.
