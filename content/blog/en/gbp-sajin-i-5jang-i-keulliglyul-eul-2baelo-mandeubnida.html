When you search for your own shop on Google, which photo do you see first?

A dark interior, an old entrance or an empty photo section can leave a customer with unanswered questions. A nearby business with clear, current images gives them more to work with before choosing where to visit.

If you have been putting off the photos since registering your Google Business Profile, begin with the five types below. They help explain the place, products and people. Can Business Profile Photos Make a Difference?

Photos can help customers understand what they are choosing. The size of any effect on your own business needs to be measured.

A Google article published on July 12, 2016 reported 35% more clicks and 42% more direction requests for listings with photos than those without, citing Google/Oxera research from December 2014. These are historical reported comparisons, not a current Korean-store experiment, a click-through-rate increase or proof that photos alone cause more sales.

Start with These Five Views of Your Business

You can build the collection a few useful photos at a time. Start with five views that answer a customer’s immediate questions. This is a practical starter selection, not a Google requirement to upload exactly five images.

1. The Exterior: Help Customers Recognize the Entrance

Show the building and sign from an angle a person approaching the business would recognize.

  • Try daylight, such as between 10 a.m. and 2 p.m., where that suits the building and weather. Check shadows rather than following the clock rigidly.
  • Make sure the business name on the sign is legible.
  • Include nearby features or a street sign when they help identify the entrance.
  • A smartphone is sufficient. A wider view can show the building, provided it does not distort the scene misleadingly.

2. The Interior: Show What a Visit Feels Like

Photograph the actual space after it has been tidied. A customer should be able to recognize it when they arrive.

  • Before opening can be a convenient time, once cleaning and preparation are finished.
  • Use the lighting customers will encounter, and make the image clear enough to see the space.
  • A diagonal view can show the layout. Avoid stretching the image to make a small room look much larger.
  • Keep useful details visible, such as the tables, menu or seating arrangement.

3. A Representative Product or Dish: Show What You Offer

Choose one product or menu item that represents the business well. One clear subject is often easier to understand than a crowded photograph.

  • Try a window with soft natural light to show the colors accurately.
  • Photograph food when it is ready to serve, or a product in the condition customers will receive it.
  • Keep the background simple enough for the item to stand out.
  • A roughly 45-degree angle is a useful starting point; choose the angle that explains the actual item best.

4. Work in Progress: Let Customers See the Service

A salon service, a coffee being brewed or a craftsperson’s hands can help explain what happens at the business. Show a real activity without making an unsupported claim about its quality or results.

  • Choose a natural moment rather than a scene that misrepresents the work.
  • Get appropriate permission before photographing identifiable customers, and protect personal information.
  • A close view of hands or tools may explain the process without showing someone’s face.

The original article described a burger shop receiving more comments from customers who had seen a photo of patties cooking. No visit count was supplied, so the account illustrates the type of image rather than a measured acquisition effect.

5. The Owner or Team: Introduce the People Customers Will Meet

A willing team member or owner can give the page a more personal introduction. Participation should be comfortable and agreed to.

  • A natural portrait in the clothing worn at work is enough.
  • You can use a smartphone in a well-lit part of the business; professional equipment is optional.
  • If the team agrees, a group photo can help customers recognize the people who work there.

Photos Worth Replacing or Reviewing

Check images that are unclear, outdated or misleading. You can remove photos you uploaded, but another person’s photo requires a different process. Do not assume you can delete every image shown on the profile.
  • Blurry or low-resolution images: replace your own uploads when they make the subject difficult to understand.
  • Unrelated stock images: use photos of the actual business and offerings. A stock photo does not document what a customer will find there.
  • Heavy filters or alterations: keep the colors and appearance representative of reality.
  • Images dominated by promotional text: check the relevant content rules and whether the image helps a customer understand the place.
  • Old interiors: update your own photos when the layout or décor has materially changed.

The Korean article described a café receiving a complaint that its heavily filtered photos looked different from the actual space. The underlying review was not supplied. The practical advice is to show the business clearly and accurately.

How to Add or Change Your Business Profile Photos

Once the photos are ready, use the account authorized to manage the correct business.

  • Find your Business Profile through Google Search or Maps, or begin at business.google.com.
  • Select the relevant business and open its photo-management option. Labels can vary by device.
  • Choose the option to add a photo and upload an appropriate image. Media must meet policy and verification requirements before appearing publicly.
  • To remove one of your own uploads, open it and use its delete control. Report another user’s image only when it appears to violate policy.
Photo specifications: follow the current Google guidance and check upload status.
Format: JPG or PNG.
Resolution: Google recommends 720 × 720 pixels; its stated minimum is 250 × 250 pixels.
File size: between 10 KB and 5 MB.
Publication: Google says photos and videos can take 24–48 hours to appear. Pending, unverified or rejected media may not be visible, so check the actual status.

Review the images after a refurbishment, seasonal change or new product launch. A quarterly check can be a useful reminder, rather than a published ranking requirement. Google does not promise a higher position simply because a photo was uploaded recently. It also does not guarantee that your chosen cover image will appear first.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a smartphone camera good enough?

Yes, for a clear view of the business you can begin with a smartphone. Clean the lens, find suitable light and hold the phone steady. A tripod can help, but is optional. Check the photo on a larger screen if you cannot tell whether the sign or product is sharp.

How many photos should I upload?

Begin with useful coverage rather than a target total. The original suggestion of ten to fifteen photos can be a manageable collection, but it is not an official ranking threshold or a verified universal upload limit. Use the five types above as a guide and add further images when they show something customers need to see.

Can I remove a customer’s unflattering photo?

You cannot directly delete someone else’s upload merely because you dislike it. If it violates Google policy, use the report option and let Google review it. Keep your own images current, but do not assume more owner uploads will force a customer’s photo lower in the display order.
