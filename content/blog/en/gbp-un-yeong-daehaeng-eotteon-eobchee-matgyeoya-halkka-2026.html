When choosing a Google Business Profile management agency, ask how it will work before comparing its sales claims. This guide covers seven practical criteria, including ownership, consent, reviews, reporting and the end of the contract.

Look for retained business ownership, policy-compliant work and clear reporting, rather than a promise of a fixed search position.

An owner may want to know who is reliable, whether the fee is worthwhile and how to recognize an unsuitable proposal. The useful starting point is the work you can assess and the control you will keep.

Google’s official local-ranking guidance describes relevance, distance and prominence or popularity. An agency’s fee or claimed special access does not buy a better organic local position.

Why the Selection Criteria Matter

1. Better Organic Local Ranking Is Not for Sale

Google says a business cannot request or pay for a better local ranking. A provider can charge for management work, but should explain that the profile service itself is available without an extra Google charge. Advertising is a separate paid service.

2. Ask What the Agency Will Work On

Accurate information and useful customer explanations are practical areas to work on. The main local factors do not reduce to a guaranteed posting formula:

  • Relevance: choose accurate available categories and describe the actual services so the business is understandable.
  • Distance: maintain the true address, branch or eligible service-area arrangement. Adding a service area or moving a pin is not a legitimate way to promise closer proximity to every searcher.
  • Prominence: help maintain trustworthy information and genuine customer feedback. A regular pattern of posts or replies is not a published guarantee of a ranking bonus.

3. A Policy Shortcut Can Create a Different Problem

Misleading reviews, unsupported keywords in a business name or an unauthorized claim may create restrictions instead of a useful service. Ask how the provider obtains approval, checks facts and handles a problem before accepting a claim about rapid results.

Seven Questions Before You Agree to the Service

1. Will the Business Retain Ownership?

Google’s third-party policy requires the business to retain ownership or co-ownership. The business decides whether the partner should be a co-owner, manager or have no access. A business-owner account with an agency invited as manager is a practical arrangement, rather than the only permitted role structure.

  • Does the agreement explain that the business keeps ownership or co-ownership?
  • Is the agency’s role appropriate to the work, with the business’s approval?
  • Is the permission-removal process clear when the relationship ends?

2. Is Consent Obtained Before the Work?

A third party needs the business owner’s express consent to claim and manage a profile. Google also requires explicit approval for replies made on the client’s behalf; verbal consent alone is insufficient. Keep the written or digital record and agree on the scope of approval.

  • Ask how written or electronic consent is recorded before a claim or management work.
  • Agree who authorizes review replies and which individual drafts need further checking, especially where privacy or a sensitive complaint is involved.

3. Are Deliverables Separated from Outcomes?

  • Pause when the proposal promises an unconditional number-one position or claims to control Google’s ranking.
  • Ask what will be done, when you will see the work and how changes will be assessed. A commitment to submit a correction is different from Google accepting it or a customer making a booking.

4. How Are Reviews Requested and Handled?

Read the actual request and reply process, rather than accepting a promise of rapid review growth.

  • Avoid: payments, gifts or discounts for reviews, incentives to revise or remove negative feedback, positive-only selection, pressure and requested review wording.
  • Look for: optional requests to genuine customers and factual, relevant replies that protect private information. A negative opinion is not automatically a removable policy violation.

5. Can You See the Monthly Work Plan?

A useful plan describes responsibility, quantities and approvals without treating them as Google ranking quotas.

  • How relevant category and service changes will be checked and submitted.
  • What photos and posts will be created or uploaded, and who supplies the material.
  • What a response target such as 24–48 hours means: assessment, draft, approval or publication. Confirm question-management features actually available; Google ended its former Q&A API on November 3, 2025.
  • Who handles incorrect information, possible policy violations and approved permission changes.

6. Does the Report Explain Work and Results Together?

Request a sample with clear dates, sources and definitions. Google performance measures can include advertising activity, and profile actions are not the same as completed outcomes.

  • Work completed: which details changed and what was prepared or published.
  • Observed measures: available views, call clicks, direction requests and website clicks, with connected calls, visits or inquiries recorded separately where possible.
  • Next actions: what the evidence suggests improving, with unresolved questions and attribution limits.

7. Can You End the Service and Regain Control?

Under Google’s third-party policy, within seven business days of client notice the provider must allow separation from its service and the client’s exclusive control, relinquishing applicable permissions. The original table described a deadline after contract termination and a blanket data handover; the access rule should not be extended into a universal asset-transfer or refund promise.

  • Specify the access-return procedure and timing, including the policy requirement that starts with notice.
  • Define the handover of created photos, posts, templates and reports, with the rights and files actually available.
  • Clarify any contractual charges or restrictions in advance. Do not assume reviews can be exported and transferred to another business or that Google’s rule settles every contract term.

When to Ask More Questions Before Signing

  • A sales claim of a special connection to Google employees without a clear, verifiable explanation of its relevance.
  • An unconditional promise to be first within a month.
  • No business-owner access or a refusal to explain the agency’s permissions. Use invitations rather than sharing a password.
  • No clear process for ending the service and removing the provider’s access.
  • Paid or incentivized reviews, suppression of negative feedback or a blanket review-removal promise.
  • Graphs without the actual work, reporting definitions or comparison periods.

Use Local Evidence to Decide What Work Is Useful

A common template can organize a provider’s work, but the information needs to belong to the actual business and location.

  • A customer’s question depends on the service and where they are searching. Check actual questions and relevant available search information rather than assuming demand from a neighborhood name.
  • A restaurant, clinic and academy need different information and approvals. Medical qualifications, private patient details and service claims require appropriate care, not a copied promotional paragraph.
  • Write the useful answer in natural language. Repeating a location phrase or inserting it into every reply does not ensure visibility or commercial success.

Ask the agency to explain why a proposed change helps the customer and what evidence will be used to assess it.

Choose a Service You Can Assess Over Time

Before accepting a polished presentation, check these three practical points:

  • The business retains ownership and knows how access works.
  • Reviews and content are handled with consent, accurate facts and appropriate policy checks.
  • Regular reports connect completed work, observed measures and the next useful action.

Frequently Asked Questions

Will Hiring an Agency Raise My Ranking Immediately?

There is no universal immediate result or minimum number of weeks that guarantees one. Check the starting condition, actual work and comparable measures. Google’s factors and review processes still apply.

Can an Agency Be an Owner?

The business must retain ownership or co-ownership and decide the partner’s appropriate role. Manager access is often a sensible way to delegate regular work. An agency being an approved co-owner is different from it holding sole control while the business has none.

Should I Focus on Increasing Review Numbers Quickly?

Focus on genuine experiences and optional, neutral requests. Artificial volume, incentives and selective positive requests are not suitable shortcuts. Replies should address the actual feedback rather than serve as keyword placements.

Can I Rely on a Top-Placement Guarantee?

Ask what exactly is guaranteed. Defined work can be a contractual commitment; Google’s organic ranking is not a position an agency can buy or reserve. Paid advertising placement is a separate matter.

Why Use an Agency if I Could Do the Work Myself?

You may not need one. Consider the time, access and expertise required for your actual workload. A provider can be useful when its agreed responsibilities solve a real gap, rather than because every business needs a retainer.

What Should I Obtain Before Signing?

Request a monthly work plan, sample report, ownership-and-permissions explanation, and cancellation/handover terms. Confirm the fee and approval requirements as well, so that both sides understand the proposed service.
