When reviewing a hospital website design, photographs and the homepage tend to catch your eye first. But if the hospital introduction, doctor profiles and service information are all contained in one long image, visitors may struggle to find a detail again. Alongside the design, decide which information belongs on which page. If AI search is part of the plan, put hospital-approved information in the page text and make the relevant guidance easy to find.

Organize pages around the questions visitors need answered

The hospital introduction should explain the basic facts and how the hospital operates. Medical team pages should contain verified information about people who currently work there. Service pages should describe the care you actually provide and how patients can access those services. Three menu labels will not help if all three sections repeat the same promotional copy.

Write one sentence describing the question each page should answer. Where is the hospital and how does it operate? Who provides care? What should someone check before visiting? These distinctions make it easier to request the right materials. Combine information that belongs together rather than creating separate pages for topics with very little to say.

Ask the web agency for a content plan showing each page's purpose, the information to include and the person at the hospital who will confirm it. Important guidance should have its own shareable URL. Someone arriving directly at that address should be able to identify the hospital and the subject without going through the homepage.

Start medical team and service information with approved materials

Before writing a profile, have the hospital verify the source documents for the person's name, role, areas of practice, experience and qualifications. Even a supplied biography needs fresh approval if the person's employment or the details approved for publication have changed. Do not add unverified specialist credentials, affiliations or awards to make a profile sound more impressive.

Service information should also reflect what the hospital currently provides and how it operates. Leave out treatment-outcome or superiority claims the hospital has not approved, and do not write as though a webpage can replace an individual diagnosis or treatment decision. Name authors or reviewers only when their actual participation and role have been confirmed. A doctor's appearance on the medical team page does not make them the reviewer of an article.

If materials are spread across departments, discuss the page plan and internal approval process through BlinkAd's hospital website consultation. Bringing together your current materials and the people responsible for checking them makes the production scope easier to discuss.

Keep important explanations in readable text and useful links

Include opening hours and medical team information as text in the page, rather than placing them only inside images. Use photographs and diagrams to support the explanation. Visitors should not have to enlarge an image or download a file to read essential guidance.

Google's guidance on AI features in Search explains that established SEO principles also apply to AI Overviews and AI Mode, without additional technical requirements. Its recommendations include making important information available as text and discoverable through internal links. Use the handover records to verify what was implemented, then check actual search results and AI answers separately after publication.

Review the links from the hospital introduction to the medical team, and from service information to visiting procedures. Instead of repeating “Read more,” tell people what comes next, such as “Meet the medical team” or “Opening hours and visiting procedures.” Google's link guidance recommends links with actual destination URLs and wording that describes the linked page. Ask the agency to confirm that search engines can follow the links to your main pages.

Compare the approved copy with the actual pages

Before launch, review the approved text alongside the website itself. Check the hospital name and address, medical team affiliations, services and opening hours against the source materials. If the site also supplies information to search engines through structured data, ask the agency to confirm that it matches what visitors can see.

Useful handover materials include a list of page URLs, the approved copy, review records showing who checked what, and editing instructions. Mark which pages need updating when staff or service schedules change. Making information searchable continues after launch: name the person responsible for identifying and correcting changed facts so the guidance stays accurate over time.
