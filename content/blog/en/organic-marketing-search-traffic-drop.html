If fewer visitors are arriving through search, start by checking which pages changed and when. You need to distinguish a site-wide issue from a change affecting a few articles before deciding what work to do.

When a well-read article loses visitors, it is natural to wonder whether to publish more or update what you have. As a business owner, you may also worry about inquiries and whether to continue your current marketing work. Breaking down the decline can help you make that decision.

First, check whether the decline affects the whole website

A fall in total visitors may come from changes to just a few articles. Compare the pages that previously attracted the most search visits with those that have recently lost traffic.

The next checks depend on whether most articles declined at around the same time or only those covering a particular treatment or product. For a site-wide decline, consider problems with search engine access and changes in demand for important queries. If a few articles account for the drop, start with those pages' queries and recent changes.

Google Search Console lets you examine changes in impressions and clicks by page and query. When comparing periods, keep the number of days, search type, country and device filters consistent. Search Console's Performance report

Separate lower visibility from fewer clicks

The number of times a page appears in search results and the number of clicks it receives are different measures. Looking at both makes it easier to see where the change occurred.

If impressions and clicks have both fallen, consider whether people's interests have changed or your page is appearing less often for the relevant queries. If impressions are similar but clicks have dropped, check the title and description shown in search results and how the surrounding results have changed.

Use changes in the numbers as a starting point for finding the cause. If you assume an article has lost clicks simply because it is old, you may change the wrong thing. Google also distinguishes technical problems, changes in search demand and changes to its search systems as possible causes of a decline. Google's guide to investigating search traffic drops

Similar declines can call for different work

Imagine two dermatology clinics, Clinic A and Clinic B, both publishing articles on their websites and recently receiving fewer search visits.

Clinic A assumes it needs more articles and orders new content. Clinic B first reviews the pages that lost traffic and discovers that a recently updated treatment page does not open properly on mobile phones.

Clinic B's immediate task is to make that page accessible to visitors again. Publishing more articles would leave the existing access problem unresolved. After fixing the error, it still needs to review changes in the search data to assess how much the problem contributed to the decline.

Checking for problems on existing pages can reveal work that needs attention before you add new articles.

Start with changes you have a reason to make

If a page opens normally but its explanation no longer matches your services, update that information. Correct outdated inquiry methods or service details, and check whether answers to recurring customer questions are missing.

If the content remains accurate and useful but search demand has changed, there may be little reason to start by rewriting everything. Establish what has changed, then decide on the scope of the work.

Record your changes and what happens afterwards

When you update an article, record its URL, the date and what you changed. Read it again to check whether it answers the original question more fully or corrects misleading information.

When reviewing impressions and clicks afterwards, consider both the timing of the update and changes in demand over the same period. Even if the numbers change after an edit, it is difficult to attribute the entire difference to that one update.

Organic marketing involves both new articles to prepare and existing articles to maintain. If customers already find your website, identifying which articles have changed can help you decide what to do next.
